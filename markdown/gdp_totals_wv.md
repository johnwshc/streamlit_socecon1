## GDP Totals 2017 - 2024

Key Moments in the West Virginia real GDP Timeline
West Virginia’s economy from 2000 to 2024 was 
defined by a massive structural shift from a 
coal-dominated industrial base to a natural gas boom, 
punctuated by severe job losses, economic diversification 
efforts, and record-breaking tourism revenue.

2000–2008: The peak of traditional coal and the 
dawn of the shale boom

2000–2001: Coal employment and production remain strong, 
with hundreds of active mines statewide, though long-term 
mechanization trends have already begun shrinking the 
workforce.Mid-2000s: Advances in hydraulic fracturing 
kick off the Appalachian shale gas boom in the Marcellus 
and Utica plays, initiating a state transition away 
from coal.

2012–2016: The coal downturn and economic contraction

2012: West Virginia's real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) 
begins a multi-year trend of lagging behind national 
economic growth.

2015: Natural gas production booms, but the industry 
begins shedding unneeded construction and extraction 
workers. The state falls into a deep protracted economic 
downturn, leading to the zenith of the growth gap between 
Appalachia and the rest of the nation.

2017–2019: Energy rebound and statewide GDP spike

2017: A major statewide energy rebound occurs. Output 
in the mining super sector grows considerably, driving 
the state's total GDP growth.

2018: West Virginia registers its strongest year of 
economic growth in more than a decade, largely 
fueled by natural gas pipeline construction and a 
temporary resurgence in overseas coal exports.


2020–2024: Pandemic disruption, diversification, and 
infrastructure growth

Spring 2020: The COVID-19 pandemic causes immediate 
economic shock, resulting in the loss of roughly 100,000 
jobs. Southern West Virginia's coal industry suffers severe, 
lasting declines.

2021: Total natural gas production approaches its peak, 
firmly cementing the Mountain State as a premier U.S. 
energy producer.

2023: State GDP growth nearly doubles that of the national 
level, breaking a decade-long streak of lagging economic 
expansion.

2024: The total number of coal miners in the state 
drops to approximately 15,000, down from over 
120,000 in the mid-20th century. Concurrently, 
visitors spend an all-time high of $6.6 billion, 
reflecting the massive expansion of the state's 
tourism and outdoor recreation sectors.

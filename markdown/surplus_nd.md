"SURPLUS", in BEA data, includes all capital returns: 
including profits, rents, interest 
[shared capital]. This definition comes oddly 
close to Marx's definition of "surplus value".

According to Karl Marx, surplus value is the difference 
between the total value a worker produces and the 
actual wages they are paid. So, here too, The 'rents' paid to 
the owner of the land used for the factory, the 'interest' paid 
on loans that helped capitalize production, and the net 
'profits' earned by the sale of the product after all 
inputs have been subtracted.

The lessons from this chart are interesting. Prior to the surge 
in shale oil production, industrial agriculture --- agricultural 
goods produced for the global commodities markets, such as grains, 
canola, wheat, cattle and calves, soybeans, and corn --- were
the most important source of income. 

But it was not long before the surge in oil production 
and the rise in the value of the commodities’ markets 
started to make a difference.

Financial services surpluses dominate even oil. However,
they arise due primarily because the North Dakota financial 
services industry is uniquely characterized by the 
nation's only state-owned bank and an exceptionally 
high density of community banks and credit unions. 

The sector is highly collaborative, with local institutions 
retaining a vast majority of deposits and partnering with 
the state government to drive agricultural and commercial 
development.

Industry Structure & Key PlayersBank of North Dakota (BND): 
Headquartered in Bismarck, BND holds all state funds and 
operates by partnering with local banks and credit unions 
rather than competing with them.

Community Banks & Credit Unions: North Dakota features 
roughly 90 community banks and over 35 credit unions, 
holding more than 80% of deposits in the state. Major 
regional players include Gate City Bank and 
Choice Financial Group. The towering total of financial services
surluses in North Dakota is estimated to be $1.5 trillion.
